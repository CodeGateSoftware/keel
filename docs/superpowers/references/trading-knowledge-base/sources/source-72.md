## Source 72 — Four academic papers on cryptocurrency and shariah (the māl/thaman question, engaged directly for the first time)

> **Four papers, one file**, sought specifically to close the gap §66.7 logged — *"none of the four
> [prior compliance] papers mentions crypto/Bitcoin/blockchain/stablecoins at all"* — and to test
> whether any existing scholarship answers what §71 (IIFA Resolution 237, 8/24) records the
> field's highest authority as having **declined to rule on**: whether crypto is Shariah-recognised
> *māl* (property of legal value) and a tradable item. In citation order below:
> - **[A]** Dr. Muhammad Akbar Khan & Muhammad Zamin, *"The Legal Status of 'Bitcoin' in Islamic Law
>   (A Critical Review of Some of the Existing Literature)"* (AFKAR Research Journal of Islamic
>   Studies, Vol. 2 Issue 2, Dec. 2018, 14pp) — International Islamic University, Islamabad, Faculty
>   of Shari'ah & Law. A critical literature survey, read in full.
> - **[B]** Sudais Asif, *"The Halal and Haram Aspect of Cryptocurrencies in Islam"* (Journal of
>   Islamic Banking and Finance, Vol. 35 No. 2, Apr–Jun 2018, pp. 91–101, 11pp) — **not a 112pp
>   thesis as expected; the supplied 112pp file is a full quarterly journal issue** (Journal of
>   Islamic Banking and Finance, published by the International Association of Islamic Banks,
>   Karachi) containing eight unrelated articles on Islamic banking (mutual funds, zakat, CSR,
>   Nigeria banking law, Shari'ah governance, risk transfer, a country model, a book review). Only
>   one article — pp. 91–101 — is on-topic; it was located via the table of contents and editor's
>   note, read in full, and the surrounding ~100pp were not touched. Author: Lahore College of Arts
>   and Science, Pakistan — an independent researcher/practitioner, not a faculty of Islamic studies.
> - **[C]** Naim Bin Abdul Rahaman, *"Cryptocurrency in the Perspective of Shariah"* (Master of
>   Islamic Finance and Banking research paper, Othman Yeop Abdullah Graduate School of Business,
>   Universiti Utara Malaysia, 2018, 62pp) — a genuine Master's thesis, read in full (front matter,
>   all five chapters, references).
> - **[D]** Ahmed Aliyu et al. (11 co-authors — Universiti Teknologi Malaysia School of Computing,
>   OK Blockchain Centre Sdn. Bhd., Universiti Teknologi Malaysia Faculty of Islamic Civilization,
>   Universitas Ahmad Dahlan Indonesia), *"Review of Some Existing Shariah-Compliant
>   Cryptocurrency"* (Journal of Contemporary Islamic Studies, UiTM Press, received 4 Jun 2020 /
>   accepted 19 Jun 2020, 22pp) — a peer-reviewed article, read in full. **Funded by OK Blockchain
>   Centre Sdn. Bhd. and OKWAVE Co. Limited — a blockchain-industry funder**, flagged for
>   transparency; the analysis is nonetheless appropriately critical of industry marketing claims
>   (see §72.4), not a whitewash.
>
> **Net effect:** for the first time in this KB, a source *directly* engages the *māl mutaqawwim*
> question for crypto (§72.1) and the six-traits-of-money test for *thaman* (§72.2) — closing part
> of the §66.7 gap. But the engagement is **split, thin, and in one case internally
> inconsistent** — none of the four rises to the authority or rigor of §65 (Ayub) or the IIFA
> resolution §71 records. **This does not override §71's non-ruling; it corroborates why the
> question remains open.**

### 72.0 Method note

[A] (14pp) and [D] (22pp) were read in full — both are short enough that targeted reading and
linear reading are the same thing. [C] (62pp, a genuine thesis) was read in full because its
five chapters are short and its Chapter 4 "Analysis and Finding" is exactly the māl/thaman
material this source was sought for — there was no long stretch of out-of-scope banking-product
machinery to skip, unlike §65/§67's textbooks. [B]'s supplied "112pp" file required the most
triage: it is a full journal issue, not a monograph, and only an 11pp article (pp. 91–101) is
on-topic — everything else (mutual funds in India, a zakat model, CSR in Islamic banks, Nigerian
banking law, Shari'ah governance in Bangladesh, risk-transfer fault lines, a country model for
Afghanistan, a book review) was identified via the table of contents/editor's note and never
opened.

### 72.1 ⭐⭐ The *māl mutaqawwim* question, engaged directly for the first time — but thinly, and with an internal contradiction

**[C] Rahaman (UUM Master's thesis)** is the only one of the four papers — and the first source in
this entire KB — to frame the question in exactly these classical terms. Chapter 4.2 reports **two
scholarly views specifically about whether Bitcoin is *māl mutaqawwim***:

> **View 1 (necessary/harus to use):** *"The original hukm is necessary unless there are argument
> (dalil) to prohibit it. Bitcoin is mal mutaqawwim because of the reality is that it is valuable.
> Bitcoin can be alternatives to real money even though any government does not issue it. There is
> no basis that forbids money being issued by individuals or others."* Proponents dismiss the "no
> fixed price" and "no issuer" objections as equally true of fiat and stocks, hence non-disqualifying.
>
> **View 2 (haram), attributed to "an Islamic Economic Forum" statement, four bases:**
> 1. *Gharar* (uncertainty), *jahalah*, and gambling.
> 2. ***Saddul zarai'*** (blocking the means to a prohibited end) — because it leads to usury and
>    illegal trading.
> 3. **It is NOT *mal mutaqawwim* from the perspective of *syara'*.**
> 4. Some scholars hold that prohibition is simply the more cautious position (***al-ahwat***).

**This is a direct, on-point split on exactly the question §71 records the IIFA as having declined
to resolve.** It is worth being honest about how thin the sourcing is, though: View 2 is attributed
to an unnamed "Islamic Economic Forum" statement with no citation trail, and both views appear to
be drawn substantially from the thesis's own small panel of interviewed academics at one Malaysian
university (Prof. Dr. Asmadi Mohamed Naim — Dean, Islamic Business School UUM and a Central Bank of
Malaysia Shariah Advisory Council member; three other UUM faculty) rather than named, dated,
citable fatwas or resolutions. **This is real engagement with the right question, but it is
committee-opinion-grade evidence, not an authoritative determination** — it does not carry
anywhere near the weight of a body like the OIC Fiqh Academy.

⚠️ **The thesis is also internally inconsistent, and this matters for how much weight to give it.**
Chapter 4 presents the māl question as genuinely two-sided and unresolved. But **Chapter 5's own
conclusion tips decisively toward rejection** without re-earning that conclusion from Chapter 4's
balanced framing: *"Transactions using cryptocurrencies are actually much different from real-world
transactions... If [price control by large holders] happens then the practice can be said as
maysir... the contract with the cryptocurrencies is included in the fasid (corrupt) contract."* The
author's own final recommendation is to **avoid using Bitcoin as currency** and adopt a **gold-coin
monetary standard** instead — a proposal for monetary-system reform, not a trading ruling, and
out of scope for us (§72.6). **The thesis's own verdict is not a clean "haram" finding earned by its
own analysis; it is an unresolved 50/50 in Chapter 4 that becomes an unargued "avoid it" by Chapter
5.** Treat this source as evidence that the split exists among people actively working the
question, not as a tiebreaker.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — log the *māl mutaqawwim* split explicitly, citing this as the
first KB source to frame it in classical terms, but weight it as **weak-authority, split, and
internally inconsistent evidence** — it reinforces §71's non-ruling rather than resolving it.

### 72.2 ⭐ *Thaman* by proxy: the six-traits-of-money test, and a literature survey that itself lands on "invalid" while admitting the case "cannot be ignored"

**[A] Khan & Zamin** do not use *thaman*/*māl* terminology directly, but run the substantively
equivalent test: **the six traits of money** (attributed via Davi Barker to Imran N. Hussien,
*"The Gold Dinar and the Silver Dirham"*): (1) precious metal or goods; (2) abundant and freely
available; (3) durable, does not spoil/corrode; (4) has intrinsic value; (5) functions as a medium
of exchange; (6) **value assigned by God**.

The paper's own critical assessment, working through named authors on both sides (Arno
Maierbrugger, Charles W. Evans, JP Buntinx, Dr. Rodney Wilson, Davi Barker on the pro side; Green
Rabbit citing Dr. Ahmed Kameel Mydin Meera and Sheikh Imran N. Hussein, Mohammad Mahmoud Ibrahim
Tayel, Suraya Zainudin, Luqman Nurhisam on the con side) concludes: **Bitcoin satisfies traits
2, 3, 5 cleanly, trait 1 is disputed (not literally metal/food, but arguably meets the *function*
the requirement points at), trait 4 is contested (the paper itself argues Bitcoin lacks true
intrinsic value — "if it loses its status as currency, it is nothing" — distinguishing this from
mere *market-assigned* value), and trait 6 fails outright** (Bitcoin is human-created, not a
naturally-occurring commodity). **By the paper's own count this beats paper money (2/6 — medium of
exchange and abundance only, since fiat is neither intrinsically valuable nor durable in value nor
precious-metal-based)** — yet the paper's final conclusion is:

> *"It can be concluded that the six traits required for a currency to be considered as a valid
> medium of exchange in Islam do not exist in Bitcoin... it is deduced that the strong arguments
> tend to suggest that Bitcoin as medium of exchange is invalid in Islamic law. However, the opinion
> in favour of Bitcoin cannot be ignored altogether especially when it is compared with paper
> money."*

⚠️ **This is a genuine tension the paper does not fully resolve**: its own scoring favours Bitcoin
over fiat, yet its verdict leans negative. It also **conflates the *thaman* (money) question with
the *māl* (tradable property) question** throughout — never asking separately "if Bitcoin fails as
*currency*, could it still be valid tradable property (like a commodity), the way gold is tradable
even when not serving as legal tender?" That is precisely the distinction that matters for a
spot-holding agent like ours, and this paper does not draw it.

**Fatwa roundup (useful as a compact, dated reference list):** Egyptian Grand Mufti Shawki Allam
(haram — fraud/valuation risk/tax evasion); Turkey's Diyanet (haram — speculative valuation,
untraceable, money-laundering risk); Dārul-Iftā', Deoband India (haram — "just a fictitious
currency," lacks the essential attributes of real currency); Jāmia tul Rashēd, Karachi/Pakistan
(**jurisdiction-conditional**: haram where illegal, no shariah objection where legal — a
positive-law-dependent ruling, not a substantive one); Dr. Monzer Kahf (halal — treats Bitcoin like
any other currency subject to the same exchange conditions, but the paper correctly flags this as
**under-argued**: Kahf asserts the conclusion without addressing the intrinsic-value/precious-metal
objections others raise). **None of these fatwas is IIFA Resolution 237** — none of the four papers
in this batch cites it or appears aware of an OIC-level e-currency resolution at all.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — log the six-traits framework as a second, independent proxy for the
*thaman* question (distinct from the `'illah`-test already sourced at §65.2), and log explicitly
that **this paper's own scoring favours Bitcoin over fiat while its stated verdict leans the other
way** — a documented inconsistency worth citing precisely because it shows the difficulty
honestly rather than resolving it.

### 72.3 Per-protocol assertions (PoW halal / PoS haram) — a firm ruling on thin grounds, and a real corroboration on options

**[B] Sudais Asif** distinguishes cryptocurrencies (medium-of-exchange coins) from tokens
(fundraising instruments, "an asset much like a share") and asserts both categories are "Halal in
themselves," then evaluates individual consensus protocols:

- **Proof of Work = halal**, on the reasoning that *"electricity serves as the asset backing the
  currency in question without any debt being used"* (citing Maierbrugger 2017) — no debt creation,
  a real (if unconventional) backing asset. The paper itself flags the counter-argument (Alzubaidi
  & Abdullah 2017: electricity is not a "physical existence of an asset which holds its value in the
  real world") and explicitly defers: *"further research still needs to be undertaken regarding this
  specific issue."* **Honest hedge, not a resolved question.**
- **Proof of Stake = haram**, on the reasoning that staking rewards proportional to the amount
  staked are a form of interest paid on a balance, and PoS additionally creates currency without
  the "backing" argument PoW has. **This is a firm, unhedged ruling — asserted, not deeply argued
  from classical texts.**

⚠️ **This bears directly on §65.14, which records staking as *contested, not settled* — our
existing, carefully-argued position.** This paper's PoS ruling should be read as **one more
individual voice leaning haram**, not as new evidence that settles the question. §65.14's own
framing — that a staking reward could be riba (return on a loan), a fee for real validation work
(closer to *ju'alah*/*ujrah*), or profit-sharing on a deployed asset (*mudarabah*-like) — is a more
careful three-way analysis than this paper's single-sentence riba-by-analogy. **Do not upgrade
§65.14's "contested" framing on the strength of this paper alone; log it as reinforcing the
conservative (non-staking) practice while explicitly not treating the underlying question as
resolved.** (Moot operationally either way — we do not stake.)

⭐ **A genuine, useful corroboration, unrelated to the māl/thaman question:** the paper cites the
**Islamic Fiqh Academy's 63rd Resolution** (7th session, Jeddah, 9–14 May 1992) on options
verbatim: *"Option contracts as currently applied in the world financial markets are a new type of
contract which do not come under any one of the Shariah nominate contracts. Since the object of the
contract is neither a sum of money nor a utility or a financial right which may be waived, then the
contract is not permissible in Shariah."* **This is a different, earlier IIFA resolution from
Resolution 237 (§71) — on options, not electronic currencies — but it is a real, dated, named
authoritative citation**, and it independently corroborates §65.11's option exclusion (*"all agree
that these have no place in Islamic finance"*) with a primary citation this KB did not previously
have on file. The paper's own futures-contract analysis (walking CBOE's cash-settled Bitcoin
futures through the five classical conditions for a valid sale) reaches the same conclusion our
existing derivatives exclusion already holds: cash-settlement-by-price-difference is maisir,
independent of whether the underlying commodity exists.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — (a) log the IIFA 63rd Resolution (1992, options) as a second named
OIC Fiqh Academy citation alongside Res. 53/4-6 (qabd, §67.1) and Res. 237 (e-currencies, §71) —
useful for a citation-dense options-exclusion footnote; (b) log the PoW/PoS split as **one more
data point on an already-contested question**, explicitly not a resolution — no change to the
"we neither mine nor stake" posture, which remains grounded in §29.2 conservatism, not a ruling
we now hold.

### 72.4 ⭐ Token-screening criteria from real "shariah-compliant" coin case studies — mostly out of scope, but the one useful case (OneGram) independently corroborates the PAXG admission criterion

**[D] Aliyu et al.** evaluate five coins/tokens that market themselves as shariah-compliant
(OneGram, NoorCoin, Bayan Token, ADAB Solution, Stellar/Lumen) against two explicit metrics —
**asset-backing (tangible)** and **regulatory-authority oversight** — plus a third, implicit one,
**unlawful-industry/KYC screening**. Findings (Table 1 in the source): only **OneGram** (gold-gram-
backed, references AAOIFI, lower price volatility because tied to gold's spot price) is judged
plausibly shariah-compliant; **NoorCoin, Bayan Token, ADAB, and Stellar/Lumen are each found NOT
clearly shariah-compliant** despite their own marketing — mainly for lacking real, verifiable
asset-backing or a named regulatory body, notwithstanding features like KYC checks or charity
set-asides. The related-works section also reports **no scholarly unanimity**: Abubakar et al.
(2018, cited here) found *"Muslim scholars do not have a unanimous agreement on the acceptability
of cryptocurrency. Some... believe that bitcoin does not oppose the Islamic principles... While
other scholars outrightly reject the bitcoin"* — the same split picture as §72.1/§72.2, from a
different, independent literature-review source.

**This paper is fundamentally about evaluating NEW shariah-compliant token issuance — out of scope
per this KB's standing rule** (we trade existing spot BTC/ETH/PAXG; we do not issue or evaluate new
tokens for launch). But one finding transfers directly: **OneGram is structurally the same
instrument type as PAXG — a token where each unit represents an allocated gram of physical gold —
and it is the ONLY one of the five judged compliant, specifically because of real, redeemable gold
backing.** This is an independent, real-world confirmation of exactly the admission criterion §65.5
already established for PAXG (allocated/redeemable ownership = `'ayn`, vs. an unsecured issuer
claim = `dayn`). **Nothing changes about PAXG's status — it was already admitted on this basis —
but this is a second, independent source landing on the identical criterion from a completely
different angle (industry case-study comparison rather than classical fiqh derivation), which is
worth citing as corroboration.**

⭐ **A second, general documentation point worth keeping**: this paper is a clean, concrete
demonstration that **"shariah-compliant" branding on a crypto product should never be taken at face
value** — four of five self-described shariah-compliant tokens did not hold up to a two-metric
check by the paper's own authors. This is the same posture the KB already adopted at §66.3c (the
Malaysian fatwa's "substantive-or-constructive transfer" litmus test for what "spot" actually
means) — a mirrored litmus test for coin-level compliance-marketing claims: **check the actual
backing and oversight mechanism, not the label.** Directly relevant since we do not evaluate new
tokens, but useful if the allowlist is ever revisited.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — log the OneGram case as independent, non-classical corroboration of
§65.5's asset-backed-token admission criterion; log the "don't trust the shariah-compliant label"
point as a documentation note paired with §66.3c's litmus test. No allowlist or rail change — PAXG's
status is unchanged and this paper evaluates instruments we do not hold.

### 72.5 Reconciliation with §28–§33/§56/§65–§67 and with §71's IIFA non-ruling

- **No conflict with any prior compliance source on riba/gharar/maisir/spot-settlement/qabd.** All
  four papers restate these prohibitions consistently with §28.1, §65.1, §65.2; none of them
  contradicts the settled ground (long-only, no derivatives, no margin, spot/hand-to-hand
  settlement, `haram_sector` screen).
- **§66.7's negative finding ("no source addresses crypto directly") is now partially, not fully,
  closed.** These four papers DO engage crypto directly — the first sources in this KB to do so —
  but the engagement is by individual academics, a Master's student, a practitioner, and one
  engineering-heavy team, working from secondary literature and small interview panels. **None of
  them is, or purports to be, an authoritative body-level determination.** §66.7's caution — *"do
  not assume silence implies permissibility"* — should now be paired with a second, equally
  important caution: **do not assume that a split, thin, sometimes self-contradictory academic
  literature settles the question either.**
- **Direct reconciliation with §71 (IIFA Resolution 237's non-ruling):** the IIFA — per this KB's
  understanding of §71 — considered the *māl*/tradability question "in need of further
  consideration" and declined to rule. Every one of these four papers **independently arrives at
  the same place by a different route**: [C]'s thesis reports a live, unresolved 50/50 among its
  own interviewed panel on precisely the *mal mutaqawwim* question; [A]'s literature review reaches
  an internally tense verdict (Bitcoin scores better than fiat on its own six-trait count, yet the
  stated conclusion leans against it); [D] cites a 2018 finding of "no unanimous agreement" among
  Muslim scholars generally. **This is corroboration of §71's judgment, not a challenge to it** — a
  genuinely unresolved question, worked by real scholars and researchers, that still comes out
  unresolved. Per the task's own framing: an individual academic (or a small panel, or a
  literature-review verdict) asserting a conclusion does not override the field's highest authority
  having declined to say so, and nothing in this batch is authoritative enough to attempt that.
- **No source in this batch names or is aware of IIFA Resolution 237.** [A]/[B]/[C] predate it or
  do not reference IIFA on e-currencies; [D] (2020) also does not cite it. This is itself a data
  point: as of these four papers' publication dates, the OIC-level non-ruling was either not yet
  issued or not yet part of the academic conversation these authors were drawing on.

### Net assessment

**Genuinely new, and genuinely inconclusive — which is the honest finding, not a gap in the
extraction.** For the first time, this KB has sources that engage crypto's *māl* and *thaman*
status directly rather than by analogy from general Islamic-finance texts (§28–§33, §56, §65–§67).
The engagement does not resolve the question — it reproduces, from four independent angles, the
same split the IIFA's own non-ruling (§71) points to: individual scholars, small academic panels,
and literature reviews land on both "yes" and "no," sometimes within the same paper. **No single
paper here carries the weight to move `CompliancePolicy`'s working position, which remains: we
treat BTC/ETH as tradable spot assets (not as *thaman* triggering `bay' al-sarf` in the strict
sense, though the conservative branch of that analysis — spot/hand-to-hand — is what we already do
regardless per §65.2/§66.1), we treat PAXG as `'ayn` via its allocated-gold redemption structure
(now doubly corroborated, §65.5 + §72.4's OneGram case), and we do not stake or mine (§65.14,
reinforced not resolved by §72.3).** The one clean, actionable addition is a second named OIC Fiqh
Academy citation (§72.3's 1992 options resolution) and a documentation point about not trusting
shariah-compliance marketing at face value (§72.4). **Recommendation:** the crypto-specific gap
§66.7 flagged is now better understood — richly split rather than silent — but a genuinely
authoritative source (an AAOIFI standard or a body-level resolution that actually rules, rather
than declines to) would still be the single most valuable next compliance addition, should one
surface.
