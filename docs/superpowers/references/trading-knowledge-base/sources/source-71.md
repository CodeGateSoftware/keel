> finding recorded at **§66.7** — *"not one compliance source in this KB addresses crypto directly;
> silence is not permission."* In citation order:
>

### 71.1 ⭐⭐ IIFA Resolution 237 is a **DELIBERATE NON-RULING** — verified, and it must not be softened in either direction

**This is the most important finding in this source and possibly in the compliance stream.**

The resolution's structure tells the story. It has three parts. **"First: Concept, Dealing
Mechanisms and Risks"** is descriptive — it defines encrypted digital currencies, classifies them
into three types (*"1. Coins, such as bitcoin. 2. Alternative currencies or altcoins such as light
coin, bitcoin cash, ethereum and the ripple. 3. Tokens, which are tradable and exchangeable assets
with goods…"*), notes decentralization, pseudonymity, platform/wallet mechanics, Malaysia's
licensing regime, and adoption alongside documented risk.

**"Second: Shariah Ruling"** is where a ruling would go. **There is no ruling in it.** In full:

> **Second: Shariah Ruling**
>
> After reviewing the research presented and after listening to the debates on the subject, it
> became clear that **some issues are affecting Shariah rulings on cryptocurrencies, which still
> need further consideration**, notably:
>
> 1. **What is the exact nature of the cryptocurrency; is it a commodity, a benefit, an investment
>    financial asset or a digital asset?**
> 2. **Is cryptocurrency considered by Shariah a real-valued property and a tradable item?**

And **"Third"**, the operative clause, is a referral back to research:

> **Third:** In light of the above and given the **significant risks associated with this type of
> currencies and the instability of their transactions**, the Academy **recommends pursuing research
> and studies on issues affecting its ruling.**

**Stated precisely, and this is the formulation to use everywhere:**

> **The International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the OIC convened a dedicated symposium on electronic
> currencies in September 2019, debated the matter at its 24th session in November 2019, and
> ISSUED NO RULING. It identified the two foundational questions as unresolved — what crypto's
> juristic nature is, and whether Shariah considers it *māl mutaqawwim* (real-valued property) and
> a tradable item — noted the significant risks and transactional instability, and referred the
> matter back for further research.**

**Three things this is NOT, each of which would be a misreading:**

1. **It is NOT a prohibition.** No prohibitive language appears anywhere in the text. A withheld
   ruling is not a ban. Bodies that intended prohibition (Egypt's Grand Mufti 2017, Indonesian
   Ulema Council 2021, Federal Territories/Kelantan Mufti Departments 2018 — §71.8) said so
   explicitly and gave grounds; IIFA did not.
2. **It is NOT a permission**, and it must never be paraphrased as "the Academy did not object."
   The Academy's *first* substantive act on the file was to record that it does not yet know
   whether crypto is property at all.
3. **It is NOT an oversight or an incomplete document.** The resolution follows a symposium
   convened for this exact purpose. The Academy had the research in hand, heard the debates, and
   chose to publish the open questions rather than an answer. **The non-ruling is the ruling's
   content** — an institutional statement that the question is not ripe.

**Why the second question is the one that matters to us.** *"Is cryptocurrency considered by
Shariah a real-valued property (`māl mutaqawwim`) and a tradable item?"* is not a technicality
adjacent to our premise — **it IS our premise.** Every downstream compliance result in this KB
presupposes it: `bay' al-sarf` (§30.1/§66.1) presupposes there is a valued thing being exchanged;
`qabd` (§65.4/§66.2/§67.1) presupposes there is something to possess; §65.6's *"speculation per
se… is not prohibited"* presupposes the object of speculation is licit property. If BTC is not
`māl mutaqawwim`, none of those rules apply favourably — they simply do not reach, because there is
no valid sale contract to regulate.

**⚠️ Note the direction of the citation asymmetry we now carry.** §67.1 cites **this same body**
(OIC Fiqh Academy, Res. 53/4-6) as settling `qabd` in our favour. We do not get to treat IIFA as
authoritative when it helps and silent when it does not. The intellectually honest position is:
**the Academy has ruled on electronic possession and has expressly declined to rule on whether the
thing possessed is property.** Both facts belong in the compliance documentation, adjacent to each
other.

**⚠️ Secondary observation, worth flagging against §65.1.** Clause Third names *"the significant
risks… and the instability of their transactions"* as the reason for caution. §65.1 (Ayub)
established that **ordinary price volatility is NOT gharar** — gharar is a materiality threshold on
contractual uncertainty, not on price movement. IIFA does not say "volatility ⇒ gharar," and it
should not be read as saying so; but it does treat instability as a reason to withhold, which is a
weaker and different move. **Do not use IIFA to reopen §65.1.** Record it as: the Academy's caution
is risk-and-nature-based, not a gharar finding.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — this becomes the **first documented item in the compliance
section**, above every derived rule, stated as a non-ruling. The correct operational posture is
unchanged behaviour with changed *epistemic status*: we continue to operate on the working
position that BTC/ETH/PAXG are tradable property (which §71.2 shows is the majority contemporary
view and which we adopt as a reasoned interpretive stance under §29.2), while recording explicitly
that **the highest available authority has declined to affirm it.** Our premise is a defensible
scholarly position, not a settled one. That distinction is exactly what §66.7 demanded we not blur.

### 71.2 Is crypto `māl` / `mutaqawwim`? — the majority of contemporary scholarship says yes; the Academy says unresolved

This is IIFA's open question #2, and the other two documents both answer it affirmatively — with
reasoning worth recording, because the reasoning is what would survive scrutiny, not the verdict.

**71.2a — [B] SRB's argument (the fullest juristic treatment in the KB).** SRB first sets out the
classical taxonomy of tradable things — *Māl* (property), *Manfa'ah* (usufruct), *Haqq* (right),
*Dayn* (debt), or none of the above — then surveys the schools' definitions of `māl`:

> "**MĀL** is that which people have an inclination to and is storable, retrievable for future
> use." … Hanafi definitions: *"Māl is what human instinct inclines to and is capable of being
> stored for the time of necessity"*; Shafi'i (al-Suyuti): *"the terminology Māl should not be
> construed except as to what has value with which it is exchangeable; and the destructor of it
> would be made liable to pay compensation; and what the people would not usually throw away or
> disown, such as money, and the likes."*

The critical move is the rebuttal of the tangibility objection, attributed to **Mufti Taqi
Usmani** — the most senior name invoked anywhere in this file:

> "Although some Hanafi jurists have stated that Māl must be a physical entity, **Mufti Muhammad
> Taqi Uthmani dispels this argument and states that the Quran and Sunnah have not explicitly
> defined Māl, rather, Shariah has left it to the understanding of people.** Furthermore, he argues
> that some Furū' (substantive laws) in the Hanafi school describe intangibles as Māl."

SRB then argues existence and value directly:

> "The existence of something needs not be established by pointing to it or physically outlining
> it… **cryptocurrencies can be accessed when required through one's wallet. They have a form in
> the guise of digits which represent a value that can be used and exchanged for something of
> value.**" … "**Value is a concept; something people have social concurrence on.** Value is
> something which attracts Mayl (inclination)… The digits shown as a balance in digital wallets and
> on the public ledgers represent a value in the minds of people. People have an economic
> inclination to it and have economic benefit from these cryptocurrencies. **All other issues with
> regards to volatility, laundering, black markets etc. are all external matters which need
> controls and regulation to address them.**"

That last sentence is a direct, reasoned counter to IIFA's clause Third — SRB classifies risk and
instability as *external regulatory* matters rather than defects in the thing's status as property.
Note the chronology: **SRB is April 2018; IIFA is November 2019.** The Academy had eighteen months
and a symposium, and did not adopt this line.

**71.2b — [C] IIUM confirms this is the majority contemporary position and supplies the test.**
The review reports that *"Contemporary Islamic scholars have increasingly recognised various
digital assets as legitimate forms of māl, including Bitcoin… cryptocurrencies more broadly…
digital tokens… digital currencies… and non-fungible tokens,"* and gives the qualifying criteria
as a checklist:

> "First, it must be **compliant with Shariah principles**… Second, it must possess the quality of
> **`mutaqawwim`**, defined by Shaykh Zuhayli as *'something that is stored or present and
> permissible to use'*, meaning that assets that cannot be saved or lack tangible presence cannot
> be categorised as māl. Third, it must achieve **acceptability by human custom (*'Urf*)**, with
> Jalal and Che Abdullah (2024) emphasising that **all four major schools of Islamic jurisprudence
> concur that entities acquiring value through human custom and acceptance can be legitimately
> classified as māl.**"

Plus a fourth: *"digital assets must demonstrate **multifunctionality and benefit**"* — for Bitcoin
the review lists secure ownership transfer, storability, transferability, trading potential,
payment acceptance, and blockchain-conferred transparency (Perlis State Mufti Dept., 2019).

It also reports **Rosele et al. (2022)'s six requirements** for a shariah-compliant digital asset:
**possessability, transferability, value retention, practical usability, storability, and Shariah
compliance** — the closest thing in this file to a machine-checkable admission checklist (§71.6).

**71.2c — ⚠️ But note what neither document is.** SRB closes with its own disclaimer:

> "**This is a preliminary Shariah research on cryptocurrencies and is by no means a definitive
> conclusion or fatwa.** This paper was written to develop knowledge and research on this complex
> subject from a Shariah perspective. We hope that this paper will prompt and engage global Islamic
> finance bodies, Shariah scholars and Muslim economists to analyse, comment and build upon the
> arguments expressed."

**SRB does not claim to resolve what IIFA left open — it explicitly disclaims doing so.** And [C]
is a literature *review*: it reports what others have ruled, and issues no ruling of its own. So
the honest structure of the answer is: **a broad and growing body of contemporary scholarship,
including several state mufti departments and a Bahrain-licensed advisory reasoning from Usmani,
holds crypto to be `māl mutaqawwim`; no body with cross-jurisdictional authority has affirmed it;
and the body best placed to has declined.**

**Reconciliation:** this **partially closes §66.7** and is the first affirmative, crypto-specific
support in the KB for the project's foundational premise. It **does not** upgrade that premise from
interpretation to settled ruling. **Cite §29.2's scholarly-divergence caveat alongside it** — our
position is one reasoned reading, held conservatively, among live alternatives (§71.7 documents the
bodies holding the other reading).

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — record the `māl` criteria (Shariah-compliant use · `mutaqawwim` ·
`'urf` acceptance · multifunctional benefit) and Rosele's six requirements as the documented basis
for admitting a digital asset at all, above the existing `haram_sector` screen which currently
assumes admissibility and only screens function.

### 71.3 Is crypto `thaman` (money)? — genuinely split, and the split has a direct operational consequence

§66.1 flagged this as academically open and operationally moot. **These sources make it less moot
and confirm it is genuinely contested — with named bodies on both sides.**

**The "yes, it is currency" side — [B] SRB.** SRB rules currency coins to be currency by custom:

> "From a Shariah perspective, since both types of currency coins were launched to serve as a
> peer-to-peer payment system and have been regarded as a payment system, **they will be
> currencies. The Ta'amul (common usage) and Istilah (social concurrence) among users of these
> coins is that of a currency and medium of exchange.**"

with a distinction between **'Urf 'aam (widespread custom)** — which SRB says Bitcoin has attained
through merchant acceptance — and **al-'Urf al-Khās (exclusive custom)** for coins like Litecoin
and Ripple that circulate only within their own networks. Either way, currency: *"it is plausible
to assume the formation of an exclusive custom built on a blockchain."*

**The "no, it is not money" side — [C], and this is the more senior name.** The review reports:

> "Muneeza et al. (2022) state that **Sheikh Ali Qaradaghi, Secretary-General of the International
> Union of Muslim Scholars (IUMS), argues that cryptocurrencies do not fully meet the requirements
> for classification as currency.** These requirements include serving as a medium of exchange, a
> store of value (*maḥkzunan lil tsarwah*), a general measure of acceptance (*Mi'yar li Tadawul*)
> and a stability measure (*Miqyas lil Qiyam*). **Three key factors preclude cryptocurrencies from
> being considered money according to Islamic principles: the absence of intrinsic value, anonymous
> ownership and instability due to volatility.**" … "**Bitcoin does not fulfil Shariah requirements
> for money, though many Islamic scholars continue to explore its permissibility from the
> perspective of *maslahah* (public interest). In contrast, metal-backed cryptocurrencies are
> considered Shariah-compliant if they adhere to Islamic principles.**"

Note carefully: **Qaradaghi's objection is to crypto as *money*, not to crypto as *property*.**
Those are separable, and the review keeps them separate. A thing can be tradable `māl` without
being `thaman` — that is exactly the `urudh` (tradable goods) classification (§71.3a).

**71.3a — The third position, and the one that actually resolves the operational question:
`urudh`.** [C] reports the **Securities Commission Malaysia (2020b)** framework:

> "**Digital currencies based solely on technology without underlying assets are classified as
> *urudh* rather than currency, exempting them from specific rules governing *ribawi* items and
> *bai' al-sarf* principles in Islamic finance.**"

and the parallel recognition of crypto as `urudh` (tradable goods) by SC Malaysia (2020b) and the
Perlis State Mufti Department (2019), plus Bakar (2017)'s analogy to **loyalty reward points** —
permissible to redeem or trade at market value.

**⇒ Operational consequence — this ANSWERS §66.1's open question in the direction we hoped, but
via a regulator rather than a fiqh academy.** Under the SC Malaysia framing, **BTC and ETH are
`urudh`, not `thaman`, so `bay' al-sarf` does NOT govern USDC↔BTC/ETH** — those are ordinary sales
of goods for money (`bay' mutlaq`, §66.1). Under SRB's framing they ARE currency and *sarf* would
govern. **§66.1 already established that both readings converge on the same operative requirement —
spot, hand-to-hand settlement — so our behaviour is unchanged either way.** What changes is that
the conservative assumption is now *sourced as one of two named positions* rather than a
precaution against an unexamined possibility.

**⚠️ Keep the conservative reading anyway.** The convergence argument holds only while we trade
different-type pairs at market rates with immediate settlement. It would break the moment we
touched anything same-type (a USDC↔USDT swap, a wrapped-asset conversion) where *sarf*'s
equal-value branch would bind and `bay' mutlaq` would not. **Rule to keep: assume *sarf* may apply;
require parity on any same-type exchange; require spot settlement always.** No change to §30.1 —
but it is now a documented hedge against a live scholarly split rather than a blanket caution.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — record the three-way classification (`thaman` per SRB · not-money
per Qaradaghi/IUMS · `urudh` per SC Malaysia/Perlis) and the resulting rule: treat the *sarf*
question as unresolved, and satisfy the union of both regimes' requirements (spot always, parity on
same-type).

### 71.4 ⭐ Stablecoins and asset-backed tokens — the first direct, crypto-specific rule that actually binds PAXG and USDC

**This is the sharpest practical item in the file**, and it lands squarely on two instruments we
actually touch.

**71.4a — ⭐⭐ The `ribawi`-backing test (SC Malaysia, via [C]) — DIRECTLY ON POINT for PAXG and
USDC.** [C] reports:

> "In jurisdictions like Malaysia… **the Security Commission Malaysia (2020a) has established a
> nuanced classification for digital currencies backed by *ribawi* items**… distinguishing between
> two categories based on the nature of their backing. **Digital currencies backed by *ribawi*
> items comprising gold, silver and currency are categorised as currency from a Shariah
> perspective, subjecting their trading to the principle of *bai' al-sarf*, which governs currency
> exchange transactions in Islamic finance.** Conversely, digital currencies backed by *ribawi*
> items other than gold, silver and currency are classified as *amwal ribawiyyah* (*ribawi* items),
> requiring their trading to adhere to specific Shariah requirements."

**Applied to our allowlist, this is the first crypto-specific rule in the KB that changes what we
must document:**

- **PAXG is gold-backed ⇒ it is classified as CURRENCY ⇒ `bay' al-sarf` governs PAXG trades,
  explicitly and not by analogy.** §67.2 extended the sarf/spot grounding from currency to gold by
  reasoning about gold as a classical `ribawi` commodity; §65.5 established PAXG is genuinely
  stricter than BTC. **§71.4a is the direct citation both were missing** — a named regulator
  ruling that a *gold-backed digital token* falls under *sarf*. §65.5 and §67.2 are now
  crypto-specifically corroborated rather than analogically extended.
- **USDC is fiat-currency-backed ⇒ same result: currency ⇒ `sarf` governs.** This is the clean
  citation behind §30.1's stablecoin-parity note, which until now rested on reasoning from fiat
  currency rules. **USDC↔USD must be at parity and spot; USDC↔PAXG would be a currency-for-currency
  exchange of two different types under this framing — permissible at market rate, but spot is
  mandatory and non-negotiable.**
- **BTC/ETH have no `ribawi` backing ⇒ `urudh` ⇒ exempt from *sarf* (§71.3a).**

**⇒ The one non-obvious operational read: our allowlist is NOT juristically homogeneous.** Under
this framework PAXG and USDC sit under a stricter regime (`sarf`: spot mandatory, parity on
same-type, gold's classical strictness per §65.5) than BTC and ETH (`urudh`: ordinary sale). The
per-INSTRUMENT compliance attributes §65 called for are now not just prudent but *sourced* — the
same policy cannot correctly govern PAXG and BTC.

**71.4b — [B] SRB on asset-backed tokens, and an AAOIFI citation for digital `qabd`.** SRB treats
asset-backed tokens as sukuk-like beneficial ownership:

> "These tokens are similar to Sukuk al-Ijarah and Sukuk al-Murabahah in the sense that the tokens
> represents a beneficial ownership and interest in the underlying asset. **Constructive possession
> (Qabd) of the underlying asset is realised by the possession of the token in one's digital
> wallet.** This is based on the AAOIFI Shari'ah Standard No.18 on possession which states: **'3/5
> The possession of documents, like bills of lading and warehouse receipts, issued in the name of
> the possessor or acknowledging his interest therein is deemed constructive possession of what the
> documents represent if the ascertainment of commodities, goods and appliances is attained through
> them along with ability of the possessor to undertake transactions in them.'**"

**This is the KB's first citation of an AAOIFI standard *applied to a digital asset*** and it
matters for PAXG specifically — it is the argument that holding the token constitutes possession of
the gold. **But read the standard's two conditions carefully, because they are the admission test
§65.5 asked for:** (i) the document must be *issued in the name of the possessor or acknowledge his
interest*, and (ii) the possessor must have *the ability to undertake transactions in* what it
represents. That is precisely §65.5's `'ayn`-vs-`dayn` question — **allocated and redeemable, or an
unsecured claim on an issuer.** PAXG's own design (serial-numbered allocated LBMA bars, redeemable
by the holder) is what would satisfy it; a token that is merely an issuer IOU would not, however it
is marketed. **§65.5's asset-backed-token admission criterion now has a citable standard behind it
(AAOIFI SS No. 18, 3/5) rather than resting on reasoning alone.**

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — (a) record the `ribawi`-backing test as a **per-instrument
classifier** at allowlist admission: gold/silver/fiat-backed ⇒ currency ⇒ *sarf* regime;
other-`ribawi`-backed ⇒ `amwal ribawiyyah`; unbacked ⇒ `urudh` ⇒ ordinary sale. (b) Attach
**AAOIFI SS No. 18 §3/5** to the existing §65.5 asset-backed-token admission check as its citation,
with the two conditions (named/acknowledged interest · ability to transact in the underlying)
stated as the actual test. (c) Document that PAXG and USDC sit under the stricter regime than
BTC/ETH — the policy is per-instrument, not global.

### 71.5 Exchange-held custody for digital assets — corroborated, and with the first *digital-specific* citation

§67.1 settled custodial `qabd` via OIC Res. 53/4-6, but that resolution concerns electronic
possession generally (argued against a card-settlement edge case), not digital assets. These
sources supply digital-asset-specific corroboration from two directions:

- **[B] SRB** applies **AAOIFI SS No. 18 §3/5** to hold that *"constructive possession (Qabd) of
  the underlying asset is realised by the possession of the token in one's digital wallet"*
  (§71.4b). This is a token-in-wallet argument, and its second condition — **"ability of the
  possessor to undertake transactions in them"** — is the *same live condition* §65.4 attached to
  our exchange balance (nothing may prevent taking delivery whenever desired). **Three sources now
  converge on the identical operative test: possession is the ability to dispose, not physical
  custody** (§65.4 Ayub · §67.1 OIC 53/4-6 · §71.5 AAOIFI SS 18 3/5 via SRB). The
  withdrawal-capability guard recorded at §65.4 (`execution/guards.py`) is the code-level
  expression of it, and is now triangulated.
- **[C] IIUM** documents the **regulated-exchange (DAX) framing** in Malaysian practice: the
  Securities Commission *"has established a structured approach to regulate digital asset offering
  and trading… governs the issuance of digital tokens through Initial Exchange Offerings (IEO) and
  the trading of digital assets on Digital Asset Exchanges (DAX), providing a regulated environment
  for such transactions"*; and the estate-planning framework (Abd Wahab et al., 2024) assumes
  **crypto held at a regulated DAX**, with the exchange acting as the operative custodian through
  which a nominee/trustee administers the estate. Likewise, the **Muzakarah Committee of the
  National Assembly for Islamic Religious Affairs (MKI, August 2021)** acknowledged Perak's and
  Selangor's *Irshad Hukum* permitting digital assets in business transactions **"provided they are
  conducted through registered and licensed authorities in Malaysia."**

**⇒ The pattern worth extracting: the permissive rulings are conditioned on the exchange being
REGULATED and LICENSED, not on self-custody.** Not one source in this file requires self-custody;
several implicitly assume exchange custody as the normal, contemplated case. **This corroborates
§65.4's conclusion (self-custody NOT required) from a third direction and adds a venue attribute we
had not recorded: registered/licensed status is itself a compliance-relevant property of the
broker**, alongside §65.4's segregated-custody (`'ayn` vs `dayn`) question. Coinbase is a
regulated, licensed US venue — we satisfy this — but it should be an explicit per-broker attribute,
re-checked per venue, not an assumed background fact.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` (per-broker attributes) — add **"registered/licensed with a
recognised regulator"** alongside §65.4's segregated-custody and withdrawal-capability conditions.
`execution/guards.py` — no change; §65.4's withdrawal-capability guard is reinforced, now
triangulated across three independent sources.

### 71.6 Token screening criteria — sharpens the `haram_sector` screen from *sector* to *structure*

Our `haram_sector` screen (§28.4, sharpened by §40.1/§41.1) rejects tokens by **on-chain function**
— riba/lending/yield tokens, prediction-market tokens. **[B] SRB supplies an orthogonal axis: what
the token legally REPRESENTS**, with a per-type ruling. This is the most directly actionable
screening material in the file.

SRB's six types and their rulings:

| Token type | What it represents | SRB ruling |
|---|---|---|
| **Work tokens** | Right to contribute/govern/"do work" on a chain (example given: MKR) | `Haqq` of the *al-Huquq al-'Urfiyyah* class, analogous to *Haqq al-murūr* (right of passage) — *"just as Haqq al-murūr were permitted to be bought and sold according to the majority of scholars, work tokens can also be sold on a secondary market. Thus, the trading work tokens is Shariah compliant."* |
| **Utility tokens** | Rights to services/units of service ("like API keys") | Also `Huquq` — *"permissible to trade such tokens on a secondary market **provided that the project is Shariah compliant and has passed the Shariah screening for ICOs**."* |
| **Asset-backed tokens** | Claim on an underlying asset, redeemable by sending the token to the issuer | Sukuk-like; `qabd` via wallet possession under AAOIFI SS 18 §3/5 (§71.4b) |
| **Revenue tokens** | Promise of participation in future revenues (*"typically no legal obligation… to honour such promises"*) | Depends on structure; possible if genuine equity + risk-sharing; **requires screening of core business activity AND financials, "like the screening methodology of shares"** |
| **Equity tokens** | Equity in the issuer — votes, dividends, beneficial interest | *"similar to purchasing shares"*; **requires the same share-screening methodology** |
| **Buy-back tokens** | Promise of appreciation backed by issuer repurchase-and-destroy | ⚠️ *"if the second sale is contingent on the initial sales contract and is agreed upon in one contract then there could be an element of **contract combination** which potentially could risk non-Shariah compliance."* |

**Three screening rules worth adopting, none of which the current screen expresses:**

1. **⭐ Function screening is not sufficient for *any* token carrying a claim on an issuer.** SRB
   requires equity and revenue tokens to pass **share-style screening of the issuer's core business
   activity AND its financials** — a two-part test our binary sector screen does not perform. This
   is the same standard §65.10 noted we already exceed on the *sector* half (binary rejection vs
   AAOIFI/DJIM's ~5% tolerance) — but we perform **no financial-ratio screen at all**, because our
   allowlist contains no issuer-claim tokens. **The rule to record: admitting any equity/revenue/
   claim-bearing token would require a screening capability we do not have.** That is a strong
   argument for keeping the allowlist to unbacked `urudh` coins + one allocated asset-backed token,
   which is exactly where it sits.
2. **⭐ The buy-back/contract-combination flag is a genuinely new failure mode for this KB.** A
   token whose economics depend on the issuer's committed repurchase risks being **two contracts in
   one** — a prohibition §66.5 recorded abstractly ("two mutually inconsistent contracts in one
   sale") and which now has a concrete token-design instantiation. **Screening question to add:
   does the token's value proposition depend on a committed issuer buy-back?** If yes → reject
   pending analysis. This also generalises §65.8's cleared-OCO reasoning (exactly one leg ever
   becomes a contract) to the *instrument* rather than the *order structure*.
3. **Utility/work tokens are permissible in principle but inherit the project's compliance.** SRB's
   *"provided that the project is Shariah compliant"* is precisely our `haram_sector` screen, now
   with a named juristic basis (`al-Huquq al-'Urfiyyah` are tradable where customary and
   economically valued) rather than a bare sector list. **Our screen is corroborated, and its
   rationale upgraded.**

**Plus [C]'s admission checklist**, already noted at §71.2b — **Rosele et al. (2022)'s six
requirements**: possessability · transferability · value retention · practical usability ·
storability · Shariah compliance; and **four features required for money**: `ta'āmul` (common
usage) · `iṣtilāh` (social agreement) · medium-of-exchange capability · `thamaniyyah` (inherent
monetary value). And **SC Malaysia (2020b)'s three criteria for digital tokens as `māl` within
`urudh`**: proceeds allocated to Shariah-compliant purposes · associated rights and benefits adhere
to Shariah · mixed-activity cases fall back to Shariah Advisory Council resolutions. The
mixed-activity fallback connects to §67.3's **Ghalaba 51%-majority** asset-composition standard,
logged there as not-currently-actionable — it remains not-actionable for our allowlist, but the two
now corroborate each other.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` (`haram_sector` → a two-axis screen) — **axis 1 (existing):** on-chain
function (riba/lending/yield/maisir). **Axis 2 (new):** what the token legally represents —
`Haqq`/utility & work (permissible if project compliant) · asset-backed (requires the AAOIFI SS 18
3/5 allocated-and-redeemable test, §71.4b) · equity/revenue (requires share-style business +
financial screening **we do not have** ⇒ reject by capability) · buy-back-dependent (**reject —
contract-combination risk**). Record Rosele's six requirements as the general admission checklist.

### 71.7 The prohibition camp — named bodies and their actual grounds

§29.2/§66.6 established scholarly divergence abstractly and with a live forex example. **[C] gives
the crypto-specific split, with names.** The prohibiting side:

> "The use and possession of digital assets, particularly cryptocurrencies, has been prohibited by
> several Islamic scholarly bodies and regulatory authorities. **The Indonesian Ulema Council
> (2021), the Federal Territories Mufti Department (2018) and the Kelantan State Mufti Department
> (2018) and the Grand Mufti of the Arab Republic of Egypt, Shawky Ibrahim Allam (2017), have all
> issued rulings against cryptocurrencies**, citing multiple concerns."

The grounds, as reported: **high risk of complete financial loss without recourse; `gharar`
(uncertainty/speculation) due to unpredictable price volatility; ignorance and excessive market
manipulation challenging regulatory oversight; deceptive/fraudulent practices; speculative trading
that may harm market participants; facilitation of money laundering; lack of centralized regulatory
oversight; the assessment that inherent risks substantially outweigh potential benefits; dangers
from unchecked authority of individuals issuing digital currency; and fundamental incompatibility
with established Shariah principles.**

**⚠️ Two of those grounds deserve honest engagement rather than dismissal:**

- **`gharar` from price volatility.** §65.1 (Ayub) is explicit that **ordinary price volatility is
  NOT gharar** — and Ayub is our primary citable authority (README). These fatwas nonetheless
  invoke volatility under the gharar heading. **This is a real disagreement, not a
  misunderstanding on either side, and it should be recorded as such:** we follow §65.1 (gharar is
  a materiality threshold on *contractual* uncertainty), which is the better-reasoned and
  more senior treatment in this KB, while noting that several state-level authorities reason
  differently. Do not paper over it.
- **Lack of regulation / no recourse.** This one **does not apply to us in the same way**, and the
  asymmetry is worth documenting: our venue is a regulated, licensed exchange with a known
  counterparty (§71.5), which is exactly the condition the *permitting* camp attaches (MKI 2021:
  "provided they are conducted through registered and licensed authorities"). Several of the
  prohibitive grounds are about unregulated peer-to-peer usage, not about regulated spot purchase
  of BTC on a licensed venue.

**And the conditionality clause, which is important:**

> "However, it is noteworthy that **these rulings may be subject to revision if significant
> improvements are implemented regarding bitcoin, whether from the perspective of production
> methods, price regulation, security systems in exchanges, or regulatory frameworks** designed to
> prevent exploitation by irresponsible parties (Federal Territories Mufti Department, 2018). This
> conditional approach suggests that Islamic jurisprudence maintains flexibility to accommodate
> technological innovations that can be brought into conformity with Shariah principles."

**⇒ The prohibitions are largely *conditional on circumstances*, not on the nature of the asset** —
which is a materially different posture from a categorical prohibition (contrast the treatment of
riba, which §65.1 establishes is strict-liability with no de minimis). **Record it that way.**

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — record the prohibiting bodies, their grounds, and the
conditionality clause verbatim in the compliance documentation. **This belongs in our own docs
precisely because it argues against us** — §29.2's pluggable-policy commitment is meaningless if we
only record the sources that agree.

### 71.8 The permission camp — and one condition that lands awkwardly on this project

The permitting side, per **[C]**: **Perlis State Mufti Department (2018), Perak State Mufti
Department (2021), Selangor State Mufti Department (2021), Majlis Islam Sarawak Ordinance (2021)**,
supported by the **Zakat Foundation of America (2021)**, Drs Nasrul et al. (2023), and **Darul
Uloom Zakariya** (South African seminary). Rationale: *"recognition of cryptocurrency as a valuable
asset with global acceptance… viable payment mechanisms, effective for money transfers
(particularly remittances) and suitable for asset preservation."*

**But the permission is conditional, and one of the conditions is a direct problem for us:**

> "…the permissibility of Bitcoin and other virtual currencies as exchange mediums is contingent
> upon specific conditions: **equal quantities must be exchanged for the same type, physical
> transfer must occur, transactions should not be motivated solely by speculative profit** and
> their value must be equivalent to legitimate fiat currency."

Taking these in turn:

- **"Equal quantities for the same type"** — this is `riba al-fadl` (§30.1) and we satisfy it: we
  never exchange same-type at non-parity. ✅
- **"Physical transfer must occur"** — this is `qabd`/delivery, resolved in our favour by §65.4 /
  §67.1 / §71.5 (constructive possession suffices; "physical" here is best read against the
  *no-transfer-at-all* case the Malaysian forex fatwa condemned at §66.3, not as a self-custody
  requirement). ✅ but note it is stated more strictly than our sources resolve it.
- **"Value equivalent to legitimate fiat currency"** — obscure as reported; plausibly a
  market-rate/no-manipulation requirement. We transact at market rate on a regulated venue.
  Insufficiently specified in the review to act on. ⚪
- **⚠️ "Transactions should not be motivated solely by speculative profit"** — **this is the one
  that lands on us, and it must not be quietly skipped.**

**⚠️ On the speculation condition — a genuine tension with §65.6.** §65 (Ayub, our primary
authority) established *"speculation per se… is not prohibited"* and that what makes speculation
`maisir` is **non-ownership / non-delivery / difference-settlement, not frequency or motive** — a
finding that *corrected* §28.3 and *removed* the shariah claim from our anti-scalping rail. **This
condition, as reported by [C], reintroduces MOTIVE as a criterion**, which Ayub's analysis
excludes. Our agent's entire purpose is to buy assets it expects to appreciate and sell them; under
a strict reading of "not motivated solely by speculative profit," that is exactly what is being
conditioned against.

**How to hold this honestly, without overcorrecting in either direction:**
- **Do not reinstate §28.3.** §65.6 is better-reasoned, more senior, and directly on point; the
  README already records the correction. One clause in a secondary review's paraphrase of a state
  fatwa does not overturn a 544-page canonical treatment.
- **Do not dismiss it either.** It is evidence that the motive-based reading of `maisir` remains
  live among authorities that otherwise permit crypto, and it means **the permissive rulings are
  not straightforwardly permission for a trading agent** — several were issued with *usage*
  (payment, remittance, asset preservation) in view, and one — the Perlis zakat fatwa (§71.9) —
  explicitly contemplates *trading profit*, which cuts the other way.
- **The defensible distinction, and the one to document:** our position rests on **real ownership
  of a real asset with real delivery, held for a mean ~24 days, sold by rule** — not on
  difference-settlement, not on leverage, not on non-delivery. That is the substance the
  anti-`maisir` prohibition targets (§65.6, §66.3c), and we satisfy it on every limb. **The
  anti-scalping rail remains prudential per §65.6 — but it is worth noting it also happens to keep
  us well clear of the motive-based reading, which is a second, independent reason to keep it that
  we did not have before.** Prudential, not mandated — but now doubly motivated.

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` — record the permission-with-conditions structure and the
speculation-condition tension explicitly, cross-referenced to §65.6. This is a documented
divergence, not a resolved point.

### 71.9 Zakat on digital assets — §33.1's report-only feature now has a crypto-specific formula

§33.1 established zakat as an optional report-only feature (~2.5% of market value per lunar year),
and §65 supplied `nisab` and the net-wealth base. **[C] supplies the first crypto-specific
treatment**, which also functions as indirect evidence on the `māl` question (a thing must be
property before it can be zakatable):

> "The **Perlis State Mufti Department (2019) classifies Bitcoin as *Urudh al-Tijarah* (trade
> goods) for zakat purposes.** Zakat becomes mandatory for Bitcoin holdings exceeding the
> equivalent value of **85 grams of gold** and held for **one lunar year**. The calculation for
> zakat on Bitcoin is defined as follows: **[(Lowest annual BTC value × BTC units) + annual trading
> profit] × 2.5%**… Importantly, **zakat can be paid in either currency or Bitcoin**."

Plus the **Selangor Zakat Board (2021)** and **Federal Territories Islamic Religious Council
(2024)** frameworks, *"employing formulas that account for current market valuations while
deducting associated costs before applying the standard 2.5% zakat rate."*

**Two things worth recording:**
1. **The Perlis formula is computable from data we already have** — `lowest annual price × units`
   is derivable from our cached OHLC + the per-tranche `positions` ledger, and `annual trading
   profit` is exactly what `analysis/pnl.py` already produces (FIFO realised P&L). §33.1's
   report-only feature could be implemented against the Perlis formula rather than a generic
   market-value×2.5%, **if and when it is built** — it remains deferred, and note that the
   *lowest-annual-value* basis is materially more conservative (in the taxpayer's favour) than
   spot-value-at-date.
2. **⭐ Note the quiet implication of the formula's second term.** The Perlis fatwa explicitly
   includes **"annual trading profit"** in the zakat base for Bitcoin. A body that computes zakat
   on crypto *trading profit* is, by necessary implication, contemplating crypto trading as a
   legitimate activity producing legitimate profit. **This is meaningful supporting evidence for
   §65.6 and a partial counterweight to §71.8's speculation condition — from the same
   jurisdiction (Perlis) that issued one of the permitting fatwas.**

**Module:** `CompliancePolicy` / `analysis/pnl.py` — if §33.1's zakat report is ever built, prefer
the Perlis formula (85g-gold `nisab`, lunar year, `[(lowest annual value × units) + trading profit]
× 2.5%`). Still **report-only, still deferred** — no change to that status.

### 71.10 Where the weight of scholarly opinion actually sits — the honest map

Assembling what these three documents establish, and being careful to distinguish *count* from
*authority*:

**On crypto as `māl` (property):**
- **Majority of contemporary scholarship: YES.** [C] reports scholars *"increasingly recognise"*
  digital assets as `māl`, and that *"all four major schools concur that entities acquiring value
  through human custom and acceptance can be legitimately classified as māl."* Backed by SRB's
  reasoning from Usmani, several Malaysian state mufti departments, SC Malaysia, and the zakat
  frameworks (which presuppose it).
- **IIFA: EXPRESSLY UNRESOLVED** (§71.1). The only body of cross-jurisdictional standing to address
  it declined.
- **⇒ Weight of opinion favours our premise; institutional authority has not affirmed it.**

**On crypto as `thaman` (money): genuinely split three ways** (§71.3) — currency (SRB), not-money
(Qaradaghi/IUMS), `urudh`/trade-goods (SC Malaysia, Perlis). **No majority.** The `urudh` reading
is the most operationally consequential and comes from a regulator's Shariah framework.

**On permissibility of dealing: split, geographically patterned, and conditional on both sides.**
Prohibiting — Indonesian Ulema Council, Egypt's Grand Mufti, Federal Territories & Kelantan (MY).
Permitting — Perlis, Perak, Selangor, Sarawak (MY), Darul Uloom Zakariya (ZA), Zakat Foundation of
America. **Note that Malaysia is internally split at state level**, which is a sharper version of
§66.6's Malaysia-vs-Indonesia forex divergence: the divergence is not merely cross-border, it is
*intra-national*. **Both camps attach conditions**: the prohibitions are explicitly revisable if
regulation/security improve (§71.7); the permissions are conditioned on delivery, parity, and
non-purely-speculative motive (§71.8).

**⚠️ A methodological caution on "weight of opinion."** [C] is a *literature review* — it counts
and characterises publications, and its own results section is bibliometric (40 articles, 31 of
them from Malaysia and Indonesia — see Table 7). **A count of papers is not a count of scholarly
weight, and a field dominated by two national jurisdictions is not a global consensus.** The
permissive tilt reported here is real but should be described as *"the direction of contemporary
Southeast Asian and industry scholarship"* rather than *"the majority view of Islamic
jurisprudence."* **This caveat matters more than any single finding in the file**, because it is
exactly the kind of overreach §66.7 warned against — and it applies to evidence pointing *our*
way, which is when it is hardest to apply.

### Net assessment (saturation-honest)

**This is the most consequential compliance source since §65, and it is consequential in an
uncomfortable direction.** It was sought to close §66.7's gap, and what it actually did was
**locate the gap precisely and prove it is not an artifact of our reading list.** The gap exists
because **the field has not closed it** — the International Islamic Fiqh Academy convened a
symposium expressly on this question in September 2019, debated it in session that November, and
published a resolution whose substantive content is *two open questions and a request for more
research* (§71.1). The second of those questions is whether crypto is property at all, which is
this project's load-bearing premise.

**What we gained:**
- **The first crypto-specific citations in the KB**, several of which upgrade existing positions
  from analogy to authority: **AAOIFI SS No. 18 §3/5** for digital `qabd` and for the PAXG
  allocated-vs-claim test (§71.4b, §71.5); **SC Malaysia's `ribawi`-backing classifier** which puts
  **PAXG and USDC under `bay' al-sarf` explicitly** and **BTC/ETH outside it as `urudh`** (§71.4a,
  §71.3a) — the first sourced demonstration that **our allowlist is not juristically homogeneous**;
  and the `māl` criteria + Rosele's six requirements as an admission checklist (§71.2).
- **A genuinely new screening axis** (§71.6): screen what a token *legally represents*, not only
  what it *functionally does* — with a new hard reject (**buy-back / contract-combination**) and a
  reject-by-capability rule (equity/revenue tokens need share-style financial screening we do not
  perform).
- **The actual map of scholarly opinion** (§71.7, §71.8, §71.10), with named bodies on both sides,
  the conditions each attaches, and the finding that Malaysia is split *against itself* at state
  level — a sharper instance of §66.6's divergence caveat than the forex example.
- **A crypto-specific zakat formula** (§71.9) whose inclusion of *"annual trading profit"* in the
  base is quiet but real support for §65.6's permissibility of trading for appreciation.

**What we did not gain, and must not pretend we did:** an authoritative affirmation that
cryptocurrency is `māl mutaqawwim` and tradable. **The weight of contemporary scholarship says yes;
the institution with standing to say so declined to.** Our premise is therefore a **well-supported
interpretive position**, held under §29.2's documented-conservative-reading discipline — the same
status as our `qabd` position *before* §67.1 upgraded it, and it has not received the equivalent
upgrade. Anyone reading the compliance documentation should be able to see that distinction without
digging for it.

**One tension to carry rather than resolve** (§71.8): the permitting fatwas condition permissibility
on transactions *"not motivated solely by speculative profit."* §65.6 (Ayub, primary authority)
excludes motive from the `maisir` test and we follow it — but the condition is live among
authorities that otherwise permit crypto, and it means the permissive rulings are **not
straightforwardly permission for a trading agent.** Our answer is substantive rather than
motivational — real asset, real ownership, real delivery, ~24-day holds, rule-driven exits, no
leverage, no difference-settlement — and it is a good answer. It is not an answer that makes the
question disappear.

**Recommendation for the compliance stream.** The general-Islamic-finance direction remains
exhausted (§28–§33, §65–§67). **The crypto-specific direction has just opened and is NOT
exhausted.** The highest-value next sources are **primary fatwa documents rather than surveys of
them** — [C]'s reference list supplies URLs for all of: Perlis (2018, and the 2019 zakat fatwa),
Perak (2021), Selangor (2021), the Federal Territories Bayan Linnas Siri ke-153 (2018), MUI (2021),
and **SC Malaysia's *"Digital Assets from Shariah Perspective"* (2020b)**, which is the origin of
the `ribawi`-backing classifier extracted at §71.4a and is likely the single most operationally
relevant document named anywhere in this file. A direct read of **AAOIFI Shari'ah Standard No. 18
(Possession/Qabd)** would also be worth more than another survey, since three separate KB sections
now lean on it at second hand. **What no further source is likely to supply is what IIFA withheld** —
that would require the Academy to revisit the file, and as of this resolution it has not.
